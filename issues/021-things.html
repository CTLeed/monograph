Seventeen Years
of No.

Cultured Code shipped Things 3 in 2017. They have shipped no Things 4. What does it mean to build a product that refuses to move?

Essay by The Editors Subject: Things 3 · Cultured Code Filed: Stuttgart

In an industry that rewards momentum, Cultured Code has spent seventeen years holding still. Things, their flagship task manager, was first released in 2008; the current major version, Things 3, shipped in 2017. Nine years on, it is still the current major version. There has been no Things 4, no ‘Things Pro’ subscription, no AI companion, no collaborative workspaces, no rebrand, no pivot. The product has been maintained, refined, and carried across platforms, but its shape has not substantially changed since the Obama administration.

This is, depending on your temperament, either artisanship or negligence. We think it is neither, exactly. It is an unusually disciplined answer to a question most companies never ask: what does this product want to become?

The answer, which was: nothing

Most products grow because growing is the default. A team ships a thing, the thing sells, the team hires, the team needs to justify the hiring, the thing gets bigger. After five years the original product is a layer cake of commitments made by people who have left the company to employees who would rather be shipping something else. Growth is not a strategy; it is the absence of one.

Things 3 is a refusal of this default. Cultured Code seems to have reached a version of the product that does the job, and concluded — quietly, without a blog post — that the job does not require further additions. New platforms, yes. Polish, constantly. The same product, indefinitely.

The hardest decision in software is not what to build. It is the decision, made every Monday morning, to stop building.

There is a financial reality inside this choice. Cultured Code is a small, privately held German company. They are not under pressure to hit a quarterly number. They can price the product at a one-time purchase (one price per platform, no subscription) because their cost base lets them, and because they trust their cohort’s loyalty. This is a set of conditions not available to most software companies, and that fact is worth keeping in mind when admiring the discipline. The discipline is aesthetic; the conditions are structural.

What refusal produces

Open Things 3 today and it is immediately clear what nine years of not-shipping-new-features has bought. The interaction model is watertight. The keyboard shortcuts are muscle memory for a million users. The syncing engine is invisibly correct. The type is set with care. The empty states are lovely. Nothing anywhere in the interface suggests ‘we decided this in a meeting last quarter.’

Compare any competitor that has been shipping features continuously. Todoist, a fine product, has become a commercial forest; each feature a tree, each tree seeking light, the canopy denser every year. It is no longer obvious how to use Todoist without reading a blog post. Things is still, after seventeen years, a task manager a colleague can teach you in a single coffee break.

The cost of standing still

The refusal is not free. There are legitimate things Things 3 does not do well, and every year the list grows. It does not have native collaboration. It does not integrate with calendars beyond a shallow reading. It cannot be scripted by end users without hacks. It has no web client. Its automation story is gestural at best.

Users have left because of these gaps. Reviewers have called the product stale. At some point — and this is the unresolved question — the gap between Things and its competitors widens enough that the elegance of the core experience cannot compensate. It is not clear when that point arrives, or whether it has already. We suspect Cultured Code is watching and measuring; we would not presume to know better.

On the Theodor Fontane problem

There is a line — often misattributed — that goes, roughly: ‘there is no honour in repeating yourself; there is only honour in not having changed what did not need to change.’ Things 3 is a long argument that the second clause is possible. The product has not changed because the underlying job of managing personal tasks has not changed. GTD is still GTD. Today’s list is still today’s list. Why should the tool that does this be re-written every three years?

The counter-argument is that software drifts even when it stands still. Platforms change. Operating systems change. The affordances users expect change. A product that does not move forward is actually moving backward against a current. Cultured Code has been, in their quiet way, paddling against this current — porting to new Macs, new iPads, new input methods, new widgets, new Shortcuts — without letting the paddling show. Maintaining a nine-year-old product as ‘the current product’ is harder than shipping a new one.

Things 3 does not look frozen because it is frozen. It looks frozen because no one is forcing it to hurry.

What to learn

The lesson of Things 3, for most builders, is not to imitate it. The conditions that make the refusal possible — no investors, a loyal niche, one-time pricing that cohort-funds the maintenance, a small team, a European cost base — are not general. You probably cannot do this.

But the underlying discipline is available to anyone: ask, of any feature you are about to build, whether it makes the product better or only bigger. Most features make the product bigger. Cultured Code has, for seventeen years, chosen to ship only the ones that made Things better, and to stop when the list ran out. That is not inaction. That is a stronger kind of judgment than most roadmaps display.

There will be a Things 4 eventually. We will be surprised if it is different in ways that make the product larger. We will be less surprised if it is different in ways that make the product more like itself.
