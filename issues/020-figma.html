The Multiplayer
Bet.

Figma spent six years building a collaborative design tool in public silence. The silence was the product.

Essay by The Editors Subject: Figma Filed: San Francisco

Figma is the canonical example of the long-horizon product bet in the modern software era — a company that took one idea, held it for longer than any rational schedule allowed, and was rewarded for it to a degree that still makes product people flinch. The founding observation — that design software should work the way Google Docs works — sounds obvious in retrospect, which is usually a sign that it was hard to see at the time.

It was. When Dylan Field and Evan Wallace started building Figma in 2012, the dominant design tool was a piece of Bohemian-issued desktop software called Sketch, which did a good job of the things Photoshop did poorly, and which was beloved by designers in the way dogs are beloved by their owners. Suggesting that Sketch was going to be displaced by a browser-based competitor, with no desktop install and live cursors showing your colleagues’ work in progress, was not a widely shared view. Designers — a sceptical community in all eras — were particularly suspicious of the browser.

The six-year tunnel

Figma’s founding observation implied a lot of engineering. A design tool has to feel like a native application: sub-16 ms latency on every manipulation, hundreds of vector objects per frame, complex typography, live previews. Doing that in a browser, while also synchronising state across multiple cursors without observable conflict, is a hard problem. Figma took six years to get it right.

This is the part of the story that tends to get glossed over in post-hoc analyses. During those six years there was no Figma to release. The company had investors who wanted something to invest in; it had engineers who wanted to ship; it had designers in the room asking why they could not hand the file to a client yet. The only thing keeping Figma together was the founders’ private belief that the thing they were building was going to be better than anything else on the market, and they were going to know it when it was, and not before.

Every great platform company has a period in the middle where it looks like a scam. Figma’s was six years long.

Figma launched to the public in 2016. The reaction was polite. The thesis — that designers wanted to work in a browser — still felt uncertain. It took two more years of deliberate, quiet iteration for the product to reach the quality threshold that made the switch obvious. By 2018, any design team comparing Figma against Sketch was making a serious comparison. By 2019, the comparison was over.

What the browser unlocked

The most-discussed feature of Figma — live multiplayer — was never the most important one. Multiplayer got the demo applause. What actually changed design work was the URL.

Every Figma file has a permanent, linkable address. You can paste it into a Slack thread, a JIRA ticket, an email to a contractor, a lecture deck. The person at the other end clicks the link and sees the file, at the right zoom level, with commenting rights. No install. No file version. No ‘final_v3_fixed_LATEST.sketch’.

This is a boring, infrastructural change. It is also the reason Figma won. The discipline of being on the web forced a kind of universality that the competitors’ install-and-open-file model never had to consider. Sketch files lived on one person’s laptop. Figma files lived on the internet. The internet, it turns out, is where the rest of the team is.

The Adobe near-miss

In September 2022, Adobe announced an agreement to buy Figma for $20 billion. In December 2023, the deal was abandoned after regulatory opposition. The interesting question is not whether the deal should have gone through; it is what the near-miss reveals about Figma’s position.

An incumbent pays a twenty-billion-dollar premium for a challenger only when the incumbent understands, structurally, that the challenger has made the incumbent’s product obsolete. Adobe did not buy Figma because Figma was a nice design tool. Adobe tried to buy Figma because Adobe’s product line — fifty years of desktop Photoshop, Illustrator, XD — was being re-platformed under them by a collaborative browser-native competitor. The Adobe bid was not a gesture of enthusiasm. It was a flight to cover.

The regulators killed the deal. What they left behind was a company that had to go back to work. Figma has spent the three years since the collapse shipping aggressively: FigJam matured, Figma Slides arrived, Figma Make (their recent AI authoring surface) is interesting if not yet indispensable, and an IPO is, as of this writing, pending.

Figma’s lesson is not that multiplayer wins. It is that the browser, given long enough, eats the desktop’s best ideas.

On the next step

The harder question is what Figma does now. The collaborative-design bet paid off. The product is dominant in its category. Growth now requires either entering adjacent categories (where FigJam and Slides live) or raising the price per seat in the core category. Both are possible. Neither is easy.

The adjacent-categories path risks making Figma, over time, look like a small version of Adobe — a portfolio company in which each tool is polite to each other tool and none of them is particularly loved. The price-per-seat path risks eroding the goodwill that made Figma the designer’s tool of choice in the first place. Figma’s 2024 pricing change was a careful study in how not to do this, and it still hurt.

Our guess is that the next Figma bet is not a product feature but a stance: a decision about whether Figma is the design tool or the office-productivity tool for visual knowledge work. Those are very different companies. The current Figma is trying to be both.

What to learn

Figma is a study in the arithmetic of patience. Six years of tunnel, two years of quiet post-launch, two years more before the category was obviously theirs. Ten years of consistent focus for what looks, now, like inevitability.

If you are building something that requires the underlying platform to improve to make your product viable — browser performance, in Figma’s case; LLM latency, for many companies today — the only way to make that bet work is to still be there when the platform is ready. Every other company that had Figma’s idea in 2012 gave up before 2016. Figma did not. The bet was not just in the idea. It was in the willingness to still be there.
